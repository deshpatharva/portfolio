01 — Key Concepts

Essential Terms Explained

Eight foundational concepts that underpin every large language model — from architecture to emergent behavior. Read this first before exploring the pipeline.

Architecture

Transformer

The neural network design powering every modern LLM.

Introduced in the 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need," the Transformer replaced recurrent networks (RNNs/LSTMs) for language tasks. Its key innovation is the self-attention mechanism, which allows any token in a sequence to directly interact with any other token — regardless of distance — in parallel. Modern LLMs use a decoder-only variant (like GPT), which generates text left-to-right by predicting the next token, masked so it cannot look ahead.

Preprocessing

Tokenization & BPE

How text is broken into the units a model actually sees.

LLMs don't read characters or whole words — they read tokens, which are subword fragments. Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE) starts with individual characters and merges the most frequent adjacent pairs iteratively until a target vocabulary size (typically 32K–100K tokens) is reached. The word "unbelievable" might become three tokens: "un", "believ", "able". This balances vocabulary coverage with sequence length. The tokenizer is fixed before training begins and the model never sees raw text — only token IDs.

Mechanism

Self-Attention

How every token "decides" what other tokens are relevant to it.

For each token, the model computes three vectors: a Query (what am I looking for?), a Key (what do I represent?), and a Value (what information do I carry?). The dot product of Q and K gives an attention score — higher scores mean "pay more attention here." These scores are softmax-normalized and used to weight the Values, producing a context-aware representation for each token. Multi-head attention runs this process in parallel across many "heads," each learning different relationship types (syntax, coreference, semantics, etc.).

Scale

Parameters

The learned numbers the model stores — its "memory."

A model's parameters are all of its learned weights — the numbers adjusted during training via gradient descent. GPT-3 has 175 billion parameters; GPT-4 is estimated at ~1.8 trillion. More parameters generally means more capacity to store knowledge and handle complex tasks, but also means more compute, memory, and inference cost. Parameters are stored in the model's weight matrices (attention heads, feed-forward layers, embeddings) and are fixed after training — they don't change at inference time unless the model is fine-tuned again.

Architecture

Context Window

The maximum text a model can "see" at once.

Every LLM has a fixed context window — the maximum number of tokens it can process in a single forward pass. GPT-3 had 4,096 tokens; Claude 3 supports 200,000 tokens; Gemini 1.5 Pro reaches 1 million. Longer windows let the model reference earlier parts of a conversation or document, but memory and compute scale quadratically with context length in standard attention. Techniques like sliding window attention, FlashAttention, and Ring Attention make long contexts tractable without the full quadratic cost.

Training

Loss & Cross-Entropy

The signal that tells the model how wrong it was.

During pre-training, the model outputs a probability distribution over all tokens in its vocabulary as predictions for "what comes next." Cross-entropy loss measures how far the predicted distribution is from the correct answer (the actual next token). A perfect prediction has loss = 0; a random guess over 50,000 tokens has loss ≈ 10.8. The model's weights are adjusted via backpropagation to reduce this loss across billions of examples. Perplexity (2^loss) is the human-readable form: a perplexity of 10 means the model was as confused as if it had 10 equally plausible choices at every step.

Behavior

Hallucination

When the model generates confident but false information.

LLMs are trained to predict plausible text, not to retrieve verified facts. This means they can "hallucinate" — produce fluent, confident statements that are factually wrong, cite papers that don't exist, or invent biographical details. Hallucination emerges because the model's objective is next-token plausibility, not factual accuracy. RLHF reduces (but doesn't eliminate) hallucination by penalizing incorrect outputs. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) addresses it by grounding responses in retrieved documents at inference time, giving the model a factual source to reference rather than relying on trained weights alone.

Scale

Emergent Capabilities

Abilities that appear at scale without being explicitly trained.

As models grow larger, they develop capabilities that smaller models lack entirely — not as gradual improvements, but as sudden phase transitions. Chain-of-thought reasoning, few-shot learning, code generation, and arithmetic appear to "emerge" when models cross certain parameter thresholds. The 2022 paper "Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models" documented this across ~100 tasks. The precise mechanism is debated: some researchers argue these are artifacts of evaluation metrics rather than true discontinuities. Regardless, emergence is why scaling laws (Chinchilla, GPT-4 technical report) are central to modern LLM research — bigger models can do qualitatively different things.

02 — Process

The LLM Training Pipeline

Six stages transform petabytes of raw internet text into a capable, aligned language model ready for deployment.

01
🌐

Data Collection

Petabytes of text scraped from the web, books, academic papers, and code repositories form the raw training corpus — the raw material for everything that follows.

  • Common Crawl (web)
  • Books & literature
  • GitHub code repos
  • Wikipedia + Wikidata
  • ArXiv research papers
~5T raw tokens
02
⚙️

Data Preprocessing

Raw text is cleaned, deduplicated, filtered for quality, and tokenized into subword units. Up to 60% of raw data may be discarded before a token is ever fed to a model.

  • Deduplication (MinHash)
  • Quality filtering
  • Tokenization (BPE)
  • Language filtering
  • PII removal
~60% data discarded
03
🔥

Pre-training

The model learns to predict the next token across trillions of examples via causal language modeling. Through this process it develops grammar, factual recall, reasoning patterns, and coding syntax — entirely unsupervised.

  • Next-token prediction (CLM)
  • Transformer decoder
  • AdamW + cosine LR
  • Mixed-precision (BF16)
  • FSDP / Megatron parallelism
90%+ of total compute
04
📝

Supervised Fine-tuning (SFT)

Human annotators write thousands of (instruction, ideal response) pairs. The pre-trained base model is fine-tuned on these examples, transforming a raw text-completion engine into one that follows user instructions.

  • 10K–100K labeled examples
  • Instruction-following format
  • Human demonstrations
  • Domain-specific tasks
  • Lower learning rate
~1% of pretraining cost
05
🎯

RLHF & Alignment

Human raters rank competing model responses. A reward model is trained on these preferences, then used to guide PPO optimization — nudging the LLM toward outputs humans prefer while maintaining capability.

  • Human preference rankings
  • Reward model training
  • PPO optimization
  • Constitutional AI (Anthropic)
  • Safety filtering
Weeks of iteration
06
🚀

Deployment & Serving

The aligned model is quantized, optimized for inference speed, and served through distributed infrastructure that must handle millions of concurrent requests at low latency with strict uptime guarantees.

  • Quantization (INT8/INT4)
  • vLLM / TensorRT-LLM
  • KV-cache management
  • Safety filters + rate limits
  • Continuous monitoring
Ongoing inference cost
03 — Resources

What Training Actually Costs

Training frontier models requires extraordinary compute, energy, data, and time — costs that continue to rise with each generation.

⚡
Compute
~25,000
NVIDIA A100 GPUs

GPT-4 required tens of thousands of the world's fastest AI accelerators running continuously for months. A single A100 retails above $10,000, making hardware alone a multi-hundred-million dollar investment before a single token is trained.

Relative cost: Critical
🔋
Energy
~564 MWh
LLaMA 65B training run

Training a large language model can consume as much electricity as hundreds of homes use in a year. GPT-4 is estimated at several times this figure. Energy consumption and carbon footprint are major sustainability concerns for the AI industry.

Sustainability: High concern
📦
Data
~15T
tokens (Llama 3 training set)

Datasets span trillions of tokens drawn from web crawls, books, code, and curated sources. Data quality and diversity are often more important than raw volume — better curation consistently outperforms simply training on more data.

Impact: Foundational
⏱
Time
~90 days
estimated GPT-4 pretraining run

Even with massive parallelism, training runs span months. A single failed run can cost tens of millions of dollars. Checkpoint strategies, hardware fault tolerance, and real-time run monitoring are critical engineering challenges at this scale.

Duration: Months

05 — Deep Dive

The Three Training Phases

Understanding what happens at each major phase, why it matters, and what distinguishes one phase from the next.

Phase 01

Pre-training

~90% of total cost

The model sees trillions of tokens and learns to predict the next token in a sequence. This is unsupervised — no labels, just raw text. Through this process, the model implicitly learns grammar, facts, reasoning patterns, coding syntax, and a surprising breadth of world knowledge.

Objective Minimize cross-entropy loss on next-token prediction
Architecture Transformer decoder with multi-head attention
Optimizer AdamW with cosine learning rate scheduling
Parallelism Data, tensor, and pipeline parallelism combined
Phase 02

Supervised Fine-tuning

~1% of total cost

Human annotators write thousands of (instruction, ideal response) pairs. The base model is then fine-tuned on these examples, transforming a raw "text completion" model into one that understands and follows user instructions with a consistent voice and format.

Dataset 10,000 – 100,000+ human-written examples
Format Prompt → Ideal response pairs
Duration Hours to days of additional compute
Result Consistent instruction-following behavior
Phase 03

RLHF & Alignment

Weeks of iteration

Human raters compare model outputs and rank which is better. A separate "reward model" is trained on these preferences, then used to guide PPO optimization — nudging the LLM toward outputs humans prefer while a KL penalty prevents it from drifting too far from its SFT baseline.

Step 1 Collect human A/B preference rankings
Step 2 Train a reward model on the rankings
Step 3 Fine-tune LLM via PPO to maximize reward signal
Alternative Constitutional AI (Anthropic) — model self-critiques

07 — Further Reading

Further Reading

Curated resources — papers, videos, courses, and tools — to go deeper on every topic covered in this artifact.

Overview & Introductions
  • What Is ChatGPT Doing — and Why Does It Work? (Wolfram) Stephen Wolfram's celebrated long-form explainer. Builds up from tokens and probabilities to attention and training, with no math prerequisites. Essential first read.
  • 3Blue1Brown: But what is a GPT? (YouTube) Visual walkthrough of the full transformer and GPT architecture. Part of a series — watch all four videos for a complete picture from tokens to attention to training.
  • Financial Times: Generative AI Explained Visually Award-winning interactive explainer from the FT. Scroll-driven animations cover how LLMs generate text, why they hallucinate, and what training involves — accessible to any audience.
Transformer Architecture & Attention
  • The Illustrated Transformer (Jay Alammar) The most widely referenced visual guide to the Transformer architecture. Diagrams every component — embeddings, positional encoding, multi-head attention, and the encoder-decoder structure — step by step.
  • Attention Is All You Need — Original Paper (Vaswani et al., 2017) The paper that introduced the Transformer. Sections 3 and 4 (model architecture and training) are readable without deep ML background. The architecture described here is the direct ancestor of GPT-4 and Claude.
  • The Illustrated GPT-2 (Jay Alammar) A follow-up to the Illustrated Transformer focused on the decoder-only GPT architecture used by ChatGPT, Claude, and LLaMA. Shows how masked self-attention works for text generation.
Pre-training & Datasets
  • Training Compute-Optimal LLMs (Chinchilla paper, Hoffmann et al., 2022) DeepMind's landmark paper showing that most LLMs were undertrained on data relative to their size. Introduced the "Chinchilla scaling laws" — optimal training requires ~20 tokens per parameter. Changed how frontier labs scale models.
  • Common Crawl — The Web Dataset Common Crawl is the nonprofit that provides the petabyte-scale web crawl used in nearly every LLM training run. Their data page describes what they collect, how often, and how to access it.
  • How We Trained BLOOM — Hugging Face Engineering Blog A detailed engineering post on training BLOOM (the open-source multilingual LLM). Covers dataset construction, tokenizer design, distributed training setup, and lessons learned — one of the most transparent accounts of a frontier training run.
RLHF & Alignment
  • InstructGPT — Training LMs to Follow Instructions (OpenAI, 2022) The paper that introduced RLHF to the mainstream AI community. Describes how OpenAI trained GPT-3 to follow instructions using human feedback — the foundation for ChatGPT. Clear and well-written, with readable methods section.
  • Constitutional AI — Harmlessness from AI Feedback (Anthropic, 2022) Anthropic's paper introducing Constitutional AI, the technique used to train Claude. Rather than relying entirely on human raters, the model is given a set of principles and taught to critique and revise its own outputs against them.
  • RLHF: Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (Chip Huyen) Chip Huyen's clear, practitioner-focused breakdown of the full RLHF pipeline. Covers reward model training, PPO fine-tuning, KL penalty, and the practical challenges of running RLHF at scale.
Model Research Papers
  • Llama 2 — Open Foundation and Fine-Tuned Chat Models (Meta AI, 2023) The most transparent frontier model paper. Section 2 details training data, compute, and GPU-hours. Section 3 covers the RLHF pipeline with Ghost Attention. Highly recommended for understanding the full LLM training lifecycle with real numbers.
  • Gemini: A Family of Highly Capable Multimodal Models (Google, 2023) Google DeepMind's technical report on Gemini. Describes training across text, image, audio, and video — natively multimodal from the start. Sections 2 and 3 cover training data and infrastructure on TPU v4 pods.
  • GPT-4 Technical Report (OpenAI, 2023) OpenAI's deliberately sparse technical report on GPT-4. Notable for what it doesn't disclose (architecture, parameters, data). Still useful for evaluation methodology, safety testing, and understanding why OpenAI chose not to publish details.
  • Language Models are Few-Shot Learners — GPT-3 Paper (Brown et al., 2020) The GPT-3 paper that changed the field. Introduced few-shot prompting and showed that scale alone (175B parameters) unlocked qualitatively new capabilities. Sections 2–4 cover the model, training, and evaluation.
Compute, Cost & Sustainability
  • AI and Compute (OpenAI Blog) OpenAI's analysis showing that compute used in AI training has doubled every ~3.4 months since 2012 — far faster than Moore's Law. Context for understanding why training frontier models has become so expensive.
  • Energy and Policy Considerations for Deep Learning in NLP (Strubell et al., 2019) The paper that first quantified the carbon footprint of NLP training runs. Found that training a large transformer with architecture search emits as much CO₂ as five cars over their lifetimes. Sparked the AI sustainability conversation.
08 — Reflection

Reflection

Before working through this material I thought I had a reasonable understanding of how large language models worked. I use these tools every day and had picked up a surface level picture from articles and casual reading. What I didn't appreciate was how much engineering sits beneath the surface, and how many distinct problems have to be solved in sequence before a model is actually useful.

The part that genuinely surprised me was the cost split between pre-training and alignment. I knew training was expensive, but I assumed the RLHF and fine-tuning stages were a significant chunk of that cost. Turns out pre-training absorbs over 90% of total compute, and everything that makes the model actually behave well costs comparatively almost nothing. That reframing changed how I think about the economics of AI. The raw capability is expensive. The usefulness is relatively cheap, which is why so many organizations are exploring fine-tuning open-weight models rather than training from scratch.

The data preprocessing section also hit differently once I connected it to work I do at Kroger. We spend a lot of time on data quality upstream in our pipelines because garbage in the Bronze layer causes compounding problems by the time data reaches Gold. The same principle applies here at a much larger scale. LLaMA 2's paper notes that up to 60% of raw crawled data gets discarded before training. Most people assume AI models are trained on everything the internet has ever produced. The real story is that careful filtering and deduplication matter more than volume.

Going through Constitutional AI for Claude was the other thing that stuck with me. The idea that a model can be taught to critique its own outputs against a set of principles, reducing the need for human raters at every step, felt like a genuinely different approach rather than just an incremental improvement. It makes the alignment problem feel slightly more tractable than the standard RLHF framing suggests, though I recognize there are open debates about how far that scales.

Overall this exercise gave me a much more grounded mental model of what generative AI actually is under the hood. I came in thinking of LLMs as sophisticated autocomplete. I leave thinking of them as the compressed output of an extraordinary data engineering and optimization problem, one that has more in common with the pipelines I build professionally than I initially expected.
