01 — Key Terms & Concepts

Jargon Explained

Eight foundational terms used throughout this artifact — from model architecture to explainability methods and regulation.

Core Concept
XAI
Explainable Artificial Intelligence

The field of methods and processes that make AI decisions understandable to humans. XAI sits at the intersection of machine learning, human-computer interaction, and ethics — seeking to bridge the gap between model capability and human trust.

Model Type
LLM
Large Language Model

A neural network with billions of parameters trained on vast text corpora to predict and generate human language. Models like GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, and LLaMA are LLMs. Their scale is both their strength and the root of explainability challenges.

Challenge
Black Box
Opaque decision system

A model or system where the internal logic is not visible, interpretable, or traceable. Deep neural networks are black boxes by nature — inputs and outputs are observable but the billions of intermediate calculations remain opaque even to their creators.

Method
Post-hoc
After-the-fact explanation

An explanation generated after a model has produced its output, rather than as part of the decision process itself. Most XAI techniques are post-hoc. The core concern: do they faithfully reflect what the model computed, or just produce a plausible-sounding rationale?

Technique
SHAP
SHapley Additive exPlanations

A method rooted in cooperative game theory that assigns each input feature a contribution score for a specific prediction. SHAP values are additive (they sum to the model output) and consistent — more important features always receive higher scores. Widely used in tabular ML and increasingly in NLP.

Technique
LIME
Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations

Explains a single prediction by fitting a simple, interpretable model (e.g. linear regression) to the complex model's behavior in the neighborhood of that input. Local means it only explains one prediction at a time. Model-agnostic means it works with any black-box model.

Technique
CoT
Chain-of-Thought Prompting

A prompting strategy that instructs an LLM to reason step-by-step before producing a final answer. The intermediate reasoning steps are externally visible, making the model's logic auditable without accessing model internals. Introduced by Wei et al. (2022) at Google Brain.

Regulation
EU AI Act
European AI Regulation (2024)

The world's first comprehensive AI law, passed by the European Union in 2024. It categorizes AI systems by risk and mandates transparency and explainability for high-risk applications (medical diagnosis, credit scoring, recruitment, law enforcement). Non-compliance carries fines up to €35M or 7% of global revenue.

02 — What is XAI?

Explainable AI & Why It Matters

XAI is the set of methods and techniques that make AI decisions understandable to humans — covering transparency, interpretability, and accountability.

Definition
What is Explainable AI?

Explainable AI (XAI) refers to methods and processes that allow humans to understand and trust the outputs of machine learning models. Unlike traditional software where logic is explicit, large language models distribute decision-making across billions of parameters — making it nearly impossible to trace any single output to a clear cause.

  • Transparency — the model's architecture and training process are open to inspection
  • Interpretability — humans can understand what factors drove a specific decision
  • Accountability — clear ownership when an AI system causes harm or produces errors

03 — Challenges

3 Core Obstacles to Explainability

Generative AI models create fundamental challenges that resist straightforward interpretation — and are unlikely to vanish as models scale.

01 / 03
⬛
Black Box Opacity

Decisions emerge from complex, non-linear interactions across millions or billions of parameters. No single component fully explains the output. The distributed and dynamic internal structure resists straightforward interpretation — even from the engineers who built the system.

02 / 03
🔍
Post-Hoc Explanation Gap

Explanations are typically generated after the decision, not during it. These post-hoc explanations may not accurately reflect the model's true internal reasoning. This gap can undermine trust — a challenge that persists even as models grow more sophisticated and explanation methods improve.

03 / 03
📊
Data Quality & Bias

Models trained on vast datasets inherit the imperfections within those datasets — including biases, noise, and underrepresentation. Ensuring data quality and representativeness remains crucial, as biased outputs will continue to be a significant concern regardless of model architecture advances.

04 — Validation & Metrics

How We Measure Trust & Reliability

Performance metrics give AI systems accountability. These four properties define what a trustworthy explanation actually looks like.

🎯
Faithfulness

The explanation must accurately reflect the model's actual decision process — not a plausible-sounding but fictional rationale.

Does the explanation match what the model actually computed?
🧑‍💼
Human-Groundedness

Real users — not just researchers — should be able to understand the explanation and use it to verify or challenge the model's output.

Can a non-expert understand and act on this explanation?
🔁
Robustness

Similar inputs should produce similar explanations. An explanation that changes drastically with minor input variations cannot be trusted.

Are results consistent across similar inputs and contexts?
📋
Completeness

The explanation should cover all factors that materially influenced the output — not just the most obvious or convenient ones.

Does it account for all relevant contributing factors?
05 — Solutions & Techniques

5 Industry Approaches

Leading AI organizations are actively developing and deploying these techniques to improve transparency and interpretability.

🔦 Attention Visualization

Heatmaps illustrating which input tokens the model focused on when producing each output token. These visualizations offer insight into the model's internal priorities — though attention patterns are not always a faithful proxy for causal reasoning.

GPT-4 Claude LLaMA
🧠 Chain-of-Thought Prompting

Encouraging models to "think aloud" through step-by-step reasoning before giving a final answer. Users can prompt any modern LLM to explain its process by simply asking — making reasoning accessible without requiring model internals access.

GPT-4 Claude Gemini
📐 SHAP & LIME

Model-agnostic post-hoc explanation methods. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) assigns each feature a contribution score using game theory. LIME (Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations) builds a simpler local model that approximates the complex model's behavior around a single prediction.

Research ML Pipelines
🔬 Probing Classifiers

Lightweight neural networks trained on top of frozen model representations to test what information the model has encoded internally. If a simple linear probe can predict syntactic structure from hidden states, the model has learned syntax — even without being explicitly taught it.

Research Labs LLaMA
📜 Constitutional AI

Anthropic's approach to making model behavior auditable through explicit principles. Claude is trained with a "constitution" — a set of rules the model uses to self-critique its own outputs. The reasoning process is visible and the governing principles are public, making behavior more predictable and accountable.

06 — Further Reading

Go Deeper

Curated papers, books, tools, and policy documents to explore every topic in this artifact further.

Overviews & Introductions
  • Interpretable Machine Learning (Christoph Molnar) The definitive free online book on XAI. Covers SHAP, LIME, partial dependence plots, and more with clear examples. Essential first read — no advanced math required for the early chapters.
  • Explainable AI: A Review of Concepts and Methods (Google) Google's foundational whitepaper surveying the XAI landscape across explanation types, model families, and evaluation criteria. Useful for understanding the taxonomy used by industry practitioners.
  • "Why Should I Trust You?" — LIME Paper (Ribeiro et al., 2016) The original paper introducing LIME. Section 3 explains the core algorithm clearly. Widely cited as one of the founding papers of modern model-agnostic XAI.
Methods & Techniques
  • A Unified Approach to Interpreting Model Predictions — SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017) The paper that introduced SHAP values. The authors show SHAP unifies six prior explanation methods under a single framework rooted in Shapley values from cooperative game theory.
  • Constitutional AI: Harmlessness from AI Feedback (Anthropic, 2022) Anthropic's paper introducing Constitutional AI — the technique used to train Claude. Explains how explicit principles enable auditable, self-critiquing model behavior without relying entirely on human raters.
  • Chain-of-Thought Prompting Elicits Reasoning in LLMs (Wei et al., 2022) The Google Brain paper that demonstrated chain-of-thought prompting. Shows that asking LLMs to reason step-by-step dramatically improves accuracy on complex tasks — and makes reasoning externally inspectable.
Tools & Frameworks
  • SHAP Documentation (shap.readthedocs.io) Official docs and tutorials for the SHAP Python library. Includes worked examples for tree models, deep learning, and NLP. The "Getting Started" notebook is a practical hands-on introduction.
  • IBM AI Explainability 360 (AIX360) IBM's open-source toolkit covering eight XAI methods with a unified API. Includes a "guidance system" that recommends which explanation method to use given your model type, data, and stakeholder needs.
  • Google What-If Tool A visual, no-code tool from Google PAIR for exploring model behavior across different inputs, datapoints, and counterfactuals. Works with TensorFlow and scikit-learn models directly in a browser interface.
Policy & Regulation
  • EU AI Act — Official Text (EUR-Lex) The full text of the European Union's AI Act, effective 2024. Article 13 (Transparency) and Article 14 (Human Oversight) are directly relevant to XAI requirements for high-risk AI systems.
  • NIST AI Risk Management Framework The US National Institute of Standards and Technology's voluntary framework for managing AI risks — including explainability and transparency. Widely adopted by US federal agencies and enterprises.
  • Stop Explaining Black Box ML Models — Rudin (2019) Cynthia Rudin's influential paper arguing that for high-stakes decisions, we should build inherently interpretable models rather than retrofitting explanations onto black boxes. A counterpoint worth reading alongside mainstream XAI literature.
Reflection

Reflection

Generative AI models — particularly large language models like GPT-4, Claude, Gemini, and LLaMA — are powerful yet fundamentally opaque. Their decision-making is distributed across billions of parameters through non-linear interactions that resist straightforward interpretation. This artifact maps the three core challenges standing between AI systems and genuine transparency: the black box opacity of distributed model internals, the post-hoc explanation gap where after-the-fact rationales may not reflect true reasoning, and data quality and bias embedded in training sets. Understanding these challenges is the first step toward holding AI systems accountable.

Validation metrics — faithfulness, human-groundedness, robustness, and completeness — give us concrete ways to evaluate whether an explanation is trustworthy, not just plausible. The five solution techniques shown here represent the industry's active response: attention visualization and probing classifiers expose model internals; SHAP and LIME quantify feature contributions; chain-of-thought prompting makes reasoning externally auditable; and Anthropic's Constitutional AI embeds explicit, public principles directly into training. The teal color identity was chosen to evoke clinical clarity and diagnostic precision — qualities that should define how we approach AI accountability. The model comparison table grounds the concepts in the real landscape of tools available today.
